Markets amidst Geopolitic Instability

March 2026 has been a punishing month for Indian equities, dominated by a sharp geopolitical escalation in the Middle East, a surge in crude oil prices, relentless foreign institutional selling, and a hawkish US Federal Reserve. Both the Nifty 50 and BSE Sensex have fallen sharply month-to-date, with the Nifty declining roughly 8.5% and the Sensex losing approximately 7% MTD, erasing months of accumulated gains. The sell-off deepened on March 19, when the Sensex crashed over 2,400 points in a single session — its sixth-worst day on record — as Brent crude briefly surged toward $119 per barrel following Iranian strikes on Gulf energy infrastructure and threats to close the Strait of Hormuz.

Domestically, heavy selling pressure from foreign institutional investors (FIIs), who have pulled out over ₹60,000 crore from Indian equities this month, weighed relentlessly on the broader market. The Indian Rupee weakened to an all-time low near ₹93.19 per USD, pressured by a widening trade deficit and surging import costs. 

Domestic institutional investors (DIIs) provided partial support through consistent net buying, but were unable to fully offset the magnitude of foreign outflows. Banking and energy counters offered intermittent pockets of support, while IT continued to underperform sharply, now down ~25% YTD, amid structural AI-disruption fears.

Chart of the Week: Inflation & Monetary Policy Watch

The most dramatic chart of March 2026 is the Brent crude oil price trajectory. Crude oil began the year trading near $63 per barrel and remained subdued through January. The onset of the US-Israel military campaign against Iran in late February, however, triggered a parabolic move — with Brent surging over 70% year-to-date to breach $114 per barrel by mid-March, briefly spiking to $119 on March 19 following attacks on Qatari energy infrastructure.

This oil shock represents a material terms-of-trade shock for India, which imports over 80% of its crude oil needs. The resulting import bill expansion, currency depreciation, and inflation expectations have fundamentally altered the market’s risk calculus. Unlike prior geopolitical episodes that were quickly resolved, the current conflict involves the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz — a chokepoint for 20% of global oil supply. 

The US Energy Information Administration forecasts Brent to remain above $95/bbl over the next two months before easing below $80 in Q3 2026, but this remains highly contingent on the duration of the conflict. For equity investors, this repricing of energy risk is the dominant macro variable shaping portfolio positioning and sector allocation decisions.

Broad Market View

March 2026 has been the most volatile and destructive month for Indian equities in over a year. The NIFTY 50 ended at ~23,002 on March 19, down sharply from the February close of approximately 25,100, representing a decline of over 8% month-to-date. Volatility surged dramatically, with the India VIX spiking over 21% in a single session, signalling extreme nervousness and aggressive hedging activity across the derivatives market.

The month began under pressure from global macro headwinds, including rising crude oil prices, uncertainty around US tariff policies, and a weakening rupee. However, the sell-off accelerated materially in the second week as the Middle East conflict escalated, with Iranian attacks on energy infrastructure and threats to the Strait of Hormuz pushing oil prices above $100 per barrel. On March 9, the Nifty breached key support levels, with the rupee touching an all-time low near ₹92.35. The index staged a three-day relief rally around March 16–18 — aided by bargain buying in IT and financial stocks — before the March 19 crash wiped out those gains entirely, as Brent briefly hit $119 and HDFC Bank’s chairman resigned unexpectedly.

On the flows front, foreign institutional investors (FIIs) have been aggressive net sellers, with total outflows in March exceeding ₹60,000 crore (~$8 billion), driven by rising geopolitical risk, surging energy prices, and a hawkish US Federal Reserve that held rates steady at 3.50–3.75% and projected only one rate cut for 2026. Seven FOMC members have dots suggesting no cuts at all this year, indicating the Fed can afford to remain patient. Domestic institutional investors (DIIs) provided crucial support through consistent net buying, absorbing a significant portion of foreign selling. However, DII support has been insufficient to prevent sharp directional declines, underscoring the severity of the current episode.

The INR weakened to ₹93.19 per USD as of March 19, 2026, a fresh all-time low, depreciating approximately 2.7% over the past month. The Reserve Bank of India has intervened heavily, with net forward dollar sales approaching $100 billion across onshore and offshore markets. However, persistently high energy import costs, sustained capital outflows, and broad US dollar strength have limited the efficacy of intervention. India’s crude import basket has climbed above $105–111 per barrel, adding an estimated $15–20 billion to the annualised import bill for every $10 rise in crude.

Theme of the Week: The Oil Shock Reshapes India's Risk Calculus

The defining theme of March 2026 is the Middle East oil shock and its cascading impact on Indian markets. What began as a targeted military campaign in late February has escalated into a full-scale regional conflict, with Iran’s threats to close the Strait of Hormuz and attacks on Gulf energy infrastructure creating the most severe supply disruption in decades.
For India, the consequences are multi-dimensional. As the world’s third-largest crude oil importer (4.5–5 million barrels per day, covering over 85% of total demand), every $10 increase in Brent adds roughly $15–20 billion to India’s annual import bill. With Brent having surged from ~$63 at the start of the year to above $110, the implied annualised hit to India’s current account deficit could exceed $70–80 billion, fundamentally altering inflation trajectories, fiscal balances, and corporate margins. US gasoline prices have already jumped more than 30% this month, and the ripple effects on global inflation expectations are increasingly being priced into central bank policy.

This has triggered a broad-based re-rating of Indian equities. Oil-sensitive sectors such as aviation, paints, tyres, and FMCG have seen pronounced selling. Banks face margin pressure from potential rate adjustments. Only oil & gas upstream producers (ONGC, Oil India) and defence stocks have offered relative shelter, with defence stocks up as much as 26% YTD. The Federal Reserve’s decision to hold rates at 3.50–3.75% and project only one cut in 2026 has compounded the pressure, with FOMC members increasingly hawkish on inflation risks stemming from the conflict.
For ultra-high net worth portfolios, this environment demands active risk management, selective hedging, and a focus on sectors with pricing power and lower import sensitivity. The critical variable remains the duration of the Strait of Hormuz disruption — the EIA’s base case assumes a gradual resumption of transit, but the tail risk of a prolonged closure remains a portfolio-level concern.

Expert's Opinion

Q&A with Market Strategist

Q: Markets have seen a sharp correction in March driven by geopolitical factors and oil prices. How should investors position in this environment?

“The current sell-off is fundamentally different from recent corrections because it is driven by an exogenous supply shock rather than domestic earnings weakness. Oil above $100 per barrel changes India’s macro narrative materially — widening the current account deficit, pressuring the rupee, and raising inflation expectations. Investors should reduce exposure to oil-sensitive sectors with thin margins, while selectively accumulating quality names in banking, domestic consumption, and energy where valuations have become attractive. The key variable to watch is the duration of the Strait of Hormuz disruption — if transit resumes within weeks, markets could see a sharp reversal.”

Q: How are FII and DII flows shaping the market, and is the domestic bid strong enough to absorb foreign selling?

“FII outflows exceeding ₹60,000 crore in a single month are historically exceptional and reflect a genuine risk-off episode, not routine portfolio rebalancing. DIIs have stepped in aggressively — monthly SIP inflows continue to exceed ₹25,000–30,000 crore, providing a steady domestic liquidity anchor. However, the sheer velocity of foreign selling, compounded by rupee depreciation, means domestic flows alone cannot prevent directional moves. What DIIs are doing effectively is creating floors and preventing panic-driven capitulation. For long-term investors, the current DII buying at depressed levels may prove to be highly accretive. The critical trigger for a reversal will be stabilisation in oil prices and any de-escalation in the Middle East.”
